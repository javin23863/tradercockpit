Methods: what each test can and cannot tell you

What each method measures, what it assumes and where it can mislead you.

Unseen data and selection

In-sample and out-of-sample

In-sample data is what you use to build or pick a strategy. Out-of-sample data is held back, so the test is less tangled up with the choices made while building. Each extra look at the held-back data weakens that separation.

Read the out-of-sample guide

Walk-forward analysis

Walk-forward analysis moves the line between fitting data and test data forward through time, again and again. It respects the order of time, and it shows whether a conclusion holds across many windows instead of one split.

Read the walk-forward guide

Selection bias

Test many candidates and some will look exceptional by chance. Judge the chosen result against the size of the search and how alike the candidates were. It was never the only trial.

Read the selection bias guide

Strategy validation

Acceptance conditions, consistency across time periods and a record of what was tested answer different questions. Keep the rule, the sample and the exact run attached to every pass or fail.

Read the strategy validation method

Probability of backtest overfitting

The probability of backtest overfitting (PBO) looks at how often the strategy picked in-sample fails to hold up on held-out slices, under a defined procedure. What it means depends on how the data was sliced, which candidates were in the family and which statistic picked the winner.

Deflated Sharpe ratio

The deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) adjusts how a Sharpe estimate is read for non-normal returns and for the number of strategies tried. It is a diagnostic. It promises nothing about future performance.

Stress and risk

Monte Carlo methods

Monte Carlo methods build many resampled or simulated paths under stated assumptions. What you get is a spread of outcomes. No single path in it is guaranteed to happen.

Read the Monte Carlo method

Parameter robustness

A result that exists at only one narrow setting can be more fragile than one that holds across the settings around it. A map of results across settings shows plateaus, ridges, sudden breaks and isolated spikes.

Read the parameter robustness guide

Market regimes

A regime groups bars by stated market measures or a documented model. The thresholds, the measures chosen, the sampling and how long a state is assumed to last decide what the label means. A label applied in hindsight is not a prediction.

Read the market regimes guide

Drawdown

Drawdown measures the fall from an earlier peak. One number can hide how long the fall lasted, how long recovery took and how often falls came back, so read it with the whole path.

Read the drawdown guide

Distribution shape

The average and the spread do not describe a distribution in full. Skew, heavy tails, more than one peak and sample size can change what the results mean, and tail estimates are unstable in small samples.

Read the distribution shape guide

Correlation and diversification

Correlation describes how two series moved together, under one measure and in one sample. It can change from one market regime to another, and low correlation in the past does not prove independence in the future.

Read the correlation guide

Metrics and charts

Equity curve

An equity curve tracks the running value of an account or strategy through its trades in order. Its shape shows drawdowns, recovery periods and gains bunched in time. A smooth curve in the past does not establish future performance. What it means depends on how the account is valued, the costs, the cash flows, the borrowing and how often it is sampled.

Net profit

Net profit adds up the profit and loss of the trades in the chosen sample, under its accounting rules. The total does not describe the risk along the way, how much capital it took or how concentrated the result is.

Net profit in the metrics guide

Profit factor

Profit factor divides gross gains by the size of gross losses over a defined sample. It sums up the balance of wins and losses. It does not describe trade order, how long drawdowns lasted, tail risk or how concentrated the gains are. When gross losses are zero the ratio needs special handling.

Profit factor in the metrics guide

Expectancy

Expectancy is the average result per trade, in a stated unit and with stated costs. A positive average in the past can sit beside a wide spread, skew, dependence on one regime or a few dominant trades. Make the unit, the sample and the costs clear before you compare values.

Expectancy in the metrics guide

Return to drawdown ratio

A return to drawdown ratio compares a chosen return measure with a chosen drawdown measure. The exact top, bottom, sign and annualising differ between platforms, so read the definition before the number. A high value can also come from a short sample or an unusually small drawdown.

Return to drawdown in the metrics guide
