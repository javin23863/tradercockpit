Methods/Validation foundations
Method · validation

Validation is a chain of decisions, not one green check.

Useful validation keeps the rules, the test data and the record of what was tested explicit. This page explains what common validation checks can establish, and what they cannot.

Predefined decision rules

Acceptance conditions

Core idea: evaluate a stated metric, operator, threshold, and sample without silently changing the rule after seeing the outcome.

An acceptance condition turns a research requirement into an explicit check: for example, whether a statistic is above, below, or within a specified boundary on a designated sample. The evidential value comes from knowing which rule was active, which data it used, and when the rule was chosen.

What can mislead you?

  • Changing thresholds after reading results turns a confirmation check into another selection step.
  • A passing condition does not establish robustness outside the metric and sample it evaluates.
  • Metric names, cost treatment, and sample scope must stay attached to the rule.
Execution-model sensitivity

Higher-precision retesting

Core idea: rerun the same strategy under a more detailed or less approximate execution model to see whether the earlier result depends on simulation shortcuts.

A higher-precision retest holds the research question and strategy logic fixed while increasing the fidelity of execution assumptions, price-path handling, or other simulation details supported by the testing platform. The comparison is useful because a result that changes materially under a more exact model may have depended on approximation rather than robust behavior.

What can mislead you?

  • “Higher precision” is not one universal setting; the exact model and data requirements are platform-specific.
  • A stable result under one more detailed simulation still does not prove live execution will match the backtest.
  • If strategy logic, costs, data, or sample boundaries change at the same time, the comparison no longer isolates execution-model sensitivity.
Coarse time partition

Calendar-year consistency

Core idea: partition an observed result by calendar year and inspect whether behavior is concentrated in only part of the history.

Year-by-year checks can reveal whether an aggregate result depends heavily on a small number of calendar periods. Useful summaries include the number of years represented and the share of those years with positive net outcome under one consistent accounting convention.

What can mislead you?

  • Calendar years are arbitrary boundaries and are not themselves market regimes.
  • Short samples or low trade counts per year can make percentages unstable.
  • A profitable year can still contain severe drawdowns, concentration, or fragile subperiods.
Research record

Evidence chains

Core idea: keep a record that shows exactly which question, settings, test run, data, result and validation a conclusion refers to.

An evidence chain makes a research result repeatable and checkable. Clear names, versions and saved copies stop a later file or run from being mistaken for the one that was actually tested.

What can mislead you?

  • A complete record shows what was tested; it does not prove the research method was sound.
  • A complete chain cannot eliminate data leakage, selection bias, or weak assumptions elsewhere in the process.
  • Keep the settings you have today separate from the settings that were in place when the test ran.
Interpretation rule

Keep the rule, the sample and the record together.

A pass or fail label is only shorthand. To understand it, keep the exact condition, the sample it was tested on, how the metric is defined, and the record of which result was tested.
