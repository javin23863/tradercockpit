## Read from here

You're their market. You're not educated. No one showed you. That's not an insult. It's the
starting point. And if someone really had a durable edge, why would they sell it to you? They
wouldn't.

I've spent twenty-five years watching traders search for the perfect strategy. They change the
indicator. They change the timeframe. They change the market. But they rarely change the way they
test the idea. That's the part that matters.

A clean backtest can still lie. A high win rate can still lose money. A beautiful equity curve can
hide one ugly truth: the strategy was chosen after the answer was already visible.

So let's make this simple.

Ten trades. Six winners and four losers. Each winner makes one dollar. Each loser costs two
dollars. The win rate is sixty percent, but the account still loses two dollars. The percentage
sounds good. The arithmetic says something else.

Now suppose I test S P Y on a five-minute chart, euro-dollar on a one-hour chart, and crude oil on
the daily. I split the data on January fifteenth, two thousand twenty-six. In sample, the curve
climbs. Out of sample, it falls. That change is not bad news. That change is the lesson.

What happens if I move the stop from seventy-six dollars and fifty-six cents to eighty-one dollars
and twenty cents? What happens if the spread doubles? What happens when the market stops trending
and starts chopping sideways?

I don't know yet. That's why we run the test.

Look at the drawdown. Twelve point five percent sounds manageable when it is printed in a table.
It feels different when you watch the account fall day after day. Risk is not an abstract number.
It is the price you pay to find out whether the idea survives.

Here's the uncomfortable part. A strategy can work for months, then decay. It can survive one
market and fail in another. It can pass every test you thought was important, until a better test
shows you what you missed.

Really? Yes.

That does not mean testing is useless. It means the test has to be honest. State the rule before
you see the result. Keep the evaluation period separate. Include costs. Include slippage. Include
the bad fills, the flat months, and the losses you would rather cut from the chart.

Then let the result land.

If it passes, we learn something. If it fails, we still learn something. Either outcome is useful
when the question was fair.

You do not need another promise. You need a process you can repeat on your own screen. I will show
you the chart. I will show you the numbers. I will tell you what they mean. And when I do not know,
I will say, “I don't know.”

No magic. No guaranteed return. No secret indicator.

Just the idea, the test, the result, and the truth.
