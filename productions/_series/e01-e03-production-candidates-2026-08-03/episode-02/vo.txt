## What a holdout is for
# receipt: E02-RW-C02
A holdout is a later block of prices that did not help choose the version being judged. The development block is the in-sample block; in-sample means the prices used to screen and select a version. Out-of-sample means a later price block reserved for judging that frozen version. Overfitting means fitting the rule to the development answers instead of a repeatable effect. Curve-fitting is the same danger described through parameter choices. Imagine choosing a setting after looking at the first block, freezing it, and then opening the later block only once for evaluation. That sequence protects the question. A segmented backtest is a backtest whose time blocks have separate jobs. A walk-forward design moves those jobs through time; that method belongs to Episode 07. The holdout can be a single later block or part of a declared rolling design, but its job stays the same: judge a version after the building decisions are finished. If we change the RSI threshold after seeing the holdout, the holdout has become part of development. The name on the folder does not protect the test; the sequence of decisions does.

## The SPY rule in plain English
# receipt: E02-RW-C03
The saved subject is a daily SPY Connors RSI2 rule. The close must be above its 200-day simple moving average, and the 2-day RSI must be below 5. The entry is recorded at the close. The exit is a later close above the 5-day simple moving average. The first 200 daily bars are warm-up for the long moving average. Warm-up is not a trade result; it is the history needed before the indicator has a full window. The entry timing matters too: a signal known at the close is not automatically a fill at the close in a live market. The rule choices tell us what was tested. They do not tell us whether the rule will survive the screen, cover costs, or execute at those prices in a live account.

## One recorded row shows why it stopped
# receipt: E02-RW-C05
One of the five recorded candidate rows has profit factor about 0.806 against a threshold of 1.3. Its return-to-drawdown is negative 0.420 against a threshold of 4. Its activity is about 0.392 trades per month against a threshold of 2. The visual preserves the exact six-decimal values beside those spoken approximations. Profit factor is the ratio of gross winning dollars to gross losing dollars. Return-to-drawdown relates net result to the worst measured fall. Activity prevents a tiny number of trades from looking like a complete answer. That row fails all three displayed checks. The important teaching move is to show the actual threshold beside the actual value. A number such as 0.806 has no meaning by itself; the gate turns it into a decision. I am showing one row as an example, and the five-candidate field remains the full context.

## The holdout is not a tuning bench
# receipt: E02-RW-C09
If a candidate fails the holdout, keep that result attached to that version. Do not move the threshold, change the exit, or call the same prices a fresh test. A new rule is a new candidate, and a new candidate needs a new development and holdout story. Looking at a holdout repeatedly while adjusting the version makes that block part of the building process. A distribution is the full set of recorded results. The median is the middle value after those results are ordered. A percentile is a stated rank within that distribution. Those descriptions belong beside the row count because a later analysis can summarize many candidates without replacing their identities. The current SPY evidence also leaves execution details and live validation outside its boundary. A careful lesson states exactly where the record ends, then defines what the next honest test would need.
